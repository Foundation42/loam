Learned spatial fields for real-time graphics

A level in
two kilobytes

Ambient occlusion for a whole Quake 3 deathmatch map, stored as 340 anisotropic Gaussians in 2.3 KiB — beating a same-sized volume texture by 2.4×, with C∞ smoothness and a free LOD chain.

Here is the same algorithm running in two dimensions. It starts empty. Every dot is a kernel it decided to buy.

The problem

Spatial fields are everywhere, and we store them all the same way

Occlusion. Irradiance. Material blends. Signed distance for collision. A modern renderer is full of functions of position that are expensive to evaluate and cheap to look up, so we bake them — and almost always into a dense grid. A 3-D texture, uniform cells, trilinear filtering.

Which is a strange choice, when you look at what those fields actually are. Take ambient occlusion in a deathmatch level. The map is 3.9% solid. Every shading point sits within a couple of voxels of a surface. The rest of the bounding box — the overwhelming majority of it — is either open air where nothing is occluded, or rock where nothing is lit.

A dense grid spends its memory uniformly on a field that is not remotely uniform. At the budget where a learned field is still doing useful work, the grid it is competing against has 184-unit cells and is worse than predicting a single constant everywhere.

A learned sparse field beats a dense grid when the interesting set is sparse in the domain — and loses when the field is non-trivial everywhere.

That rule is the useful part of this whole project. It is predictive, it decides in advance whether the technique is worth reaching for, and it has been measured on two unrelated pieces of geometry.

The constraint

You get each sample once

Here is what makes this different from fitting a neural field offline. A cache sample is expensive — one ambient-occlusion estimate is 16 rays × 20 marched steps, 320 fetches of the occluder. And it arrives wherever the renderer happened to ask.

You cannot shuffle it. You cannot epoch over it. It is not a dataset, it is a stream. A batch optimiser that re-reads its pool sixty times is not a slower option here; it is not an option.

And the samples are noisy. Monte-Carlo occlusion is a Binomial estimate — its standard deviation is √(p(1−p)/M), which is a half at one ray and a sixteenth at sixty-four. So the learner has to tell structure from sampling wobble, online, without ever seeing the same point twice.

How it works

Anisotropic Gaussians, born where the field surprises you

The model is a sum of anisotropic Gaussians. Each carries a centre, a lower-triangular factor of its precision matrix — so it can be an ellipsoid at any orientation, which is what lets a handful of kernels cover a wall or a vein instead of a hundred spheres — and one weight per channel.

Locality is exact, not approximate

That cutoff is the load-bearing design decision. Beyond √32 Mahalanobis widths a kernel returns exactly zero — not a small number, zero. Kernels are owned by the region holding their centre, and every shape is projected after each update so its cutoff box reaches at most one region edge.

So a 27-region gather is not an approximation of the sum over the model. It is the sum over the model, and one learning event is structurally incapable of disturbing a distant part of the level. Not "empirically small" — provably zero, and checked bitwise.

Learning: attribution, then a natural gradient

Each sample computes a residual, and that residual is shared among the kernels responsible for it in proportion to how much each contributed — normalised least-mean-squares, aᵢ = e·gᵢ / Σgⱼ².

The pretty part is the geometry. The natural gradient on a centre — the gradient in the kernel's own metric rather than the world's — collapses to Δμ ∝ −(w·a)·d. A kernel that under-reads at a point simply moves toward that point, by its share and no more. No matrix, no units to get wrong.

Topology is discovered

Nobody places the kernels. When a sample surprises the model by more than a threshold and no existing kernel covers it, a new one is born right there, weighted to answer that sample exactly. Descent then has to keep it honest everywhere else it reaches.

That is why capacity ends up where the structure is, and why the open air costs nothing: a model that predicts zero is never surprised by a field that is zero.

Multi-channel

Nine channels for the price of one

The geometry is the expensive part — a centre and a shape is nine floats, a weight is one. So a set carrying nine channels shares one centre and one shape between them and pays for the geometry once.

That is not a hope, it is a measurement. Fitting nine material channels instead of one costs 0.3% more kernels, because a birth is gated by geometric coverage and coverage knows nothing about channels. And the first channel's own accuracy actually improves slightly for having eight passengers — they are extra constraints on where a centre belongs, and they agree.

0.997kernels at 9 channels ÷ kernels at 1
18floats a kernel, against 90 for nine separate models
5×smaller than fitting each channel alone

One gotcha worth carrying away if you build this: the geometry descends on the sum over channels of each weight's attribution, and that sum grows as √C. Use the single-channel learning rate at nine channels and it diverges. Divide by √C and it is stable.

Distillation

Once it is built, it is a free oracle

Here is the idea that turned out to matter most, and it is embarrassingly simple. Once you have a trained model, sample it to train another one.

A teacher is two things no real field ever is. It is noiseless — a deterministic sum of Gaussians, so the student never confuses sampling wobble for structure. And it is unlimited — you can query it anywhere, as often as you like, for the cost of one gather instead of 320 marched rays.

Which means the student can be given a budget and told to hit it. Raise its surprise threshold and it simply stops buying kernels once it is within that of its teacher. Give it a coarser region grid and it uses bigger kernels and fewer of them.

Halving the population costs 14% of the accuracy. A quarter of the memory costs 23%. That is a dial you can turn per-platform, per-distance, per-anything.

And it does not degrade under iteration

The usual worry about distilling a distillation is generation loss compounding. It does not — it decays. The first copy costs 1.058×, the second costs 1.036×. The reason is structural: the teacher is a sum of anisotropic Gaussians, which is exactly what the student can represent. The truth is not. The first copy pays a representation cost; the second is fitting something it can reproduce perfectly given enough kernels.

So a distillation chain is a genuine LOD ladder. Every level is C∞ smooth rather than blocky, and because a weighted blend of two Gaussian sets is just another Gaussian set, cross-fading between levels is exact and costs nothing.

Quantization

A kernel ships in 6.75 bytes

Ten floats a kernel is forty bytes, and nobody ships forty bytes. The interesting question is which of those numbers actually need precision.

Starving each field to eight bits on its own, with the others left in full float, gives a clear answer:

Cost of eight bits, per field, measured alone
fieldspanadded error
centre32.0 (the whole extent)0.01872
log-width1.03610.00412
off-diagonal1.13040.00312
weight1.87410.00000

Two things stand out. The weight costs nothing at all — the field you would most expect to be fragile, because neighbouring kernels cancel. And the centre is the only expensive one, but its cost is a span problem, not a sensitivity problem: it is being quantized over the whole level.

A kernel's centre is inside its owning region by definition. Store it relative to that region instead, and the span drops by the region count for free — the kernels are already written grouped by region, so all you add is a count per region.

That lands the whole thing at 54 bits — 6.75 bytes — a kernel, at no measurable accuracy cost. A 5.9× saving, where the same budget with absolute centres costs 8.6%.

The nice surprise

An adaptive basis compresses better than a uniform one, and the log-width span is why. It measures 1.04 nats — a quarter of the range the clamp allows. An adaptive learner refines by placing more kernels, not by making them wildly different sizes, so the population stays tightly clustered in scale and never needs the dynamic range. A fixed-count basis covering the same detail would have to spread its widths, and would pay for it in bits.

Where to point it

Applications

Occlusion caches measured

The worked example above. Sparse in the domain by construction, because shading points live on surfaces and surfaces are a 2-D set in a 3-D world.

Procedural materials measured

Albedo, roughness, metalness and emissive as nine channels on one geometry — a marble's veins packed into Gaussians instead of a giant volume texture. Veins are 9–28% of the block, comfortably inside the rule.

Radiance & GI strong fit

RGB is three channels, spherical harmonics are nine or twenty-seven, and channels are nearly free. Watch the √C rate correction and normalise per band. Best where geometry is dense — contact shadows, corners, clutter.

Ray importance sampling strong fit

A distribution over directions per position looks five-dimensional, but its SH coefficients are just channels on a 3-D field. Clamp and renormalise on read, as the material path already does for its blend.

Collision proxies next

The best fit on paper — a surface is maximally sparse in the volume, distillation gives proxies at any budget, and C∞ means continuous normals for free. One trap: zero means "on the surface", and zero is what the model returns where it has learned nothing. Learn a narrow band instead, and read the surface off its crest.

Level of detail free

Falls straight out of distillation. Smooth at every level rather than blocky, exact cross-fades between them, and roughly 25× off the master end to end for about a quarter more error.

Open questions

What we would want to know next

01

GPU evaluation. The number that decides real-time viability. A lookup is currently 6.7 µs on one CPU core against a texture fetch's 9 ns — but that is a scalar gather of a 1 600-kernel master. The shipped asset is 340 kernels total, small enough for a shader to sum wholesale with no acceleration structure at all. That measurement has not been taken.

02

Narrow-band signed distance for physics. The most promising untried target, and the first one where maximum error matters more than mean. An RMS of 0.15 with a worst case of 0.6 is a proxy that lets things fall through floors.

03

Quantization-aware training, inside the distillation step. The student is already re-fitting from scratch against a free oracle, so snapping to the quantization grid costs nothing extra there. No headroom at 54 bits, where the cost is already unmeasurable — the case is below it.

04

Consolidation. An online pair plus a distilled low-resolution copy providing grounding, with surprise measured against the consolidated model rather than the live one. That is generative replay, and it is the principled way to tell "genuinely new" from "I knew this and drifted".

05

Sparse baselines. A dense grid is the thing this replaces, but it is not the best a renderer could do — nobody stores a dense grid over a level's bounding box. Probes clustered near surfaces would be a harder opponent, and a fairer one.
